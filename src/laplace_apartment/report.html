Corner Unit Heat-Up

A second-floor, northeast-corner apartment with no direct sun and two dogs inside. I model the floor slab, a sunlit courtyard slab, and the north and east walls as a linear heat network, solve it in the Laplace domain, and check the result with finite differences.

By 3 pm the room air reaches about 88 °F (about 87 °F at dog height) while it is 99 °F outside. The floor stays cooler, near 84 °F.

With blinds or curtains closed, those numbers drop to about 85 °F air and 83 °F floor. The 8-inch slab is the reason the room lags so far behind the outdoors. It soaks up heat from the room all day, and the garage heat moving up through it mostly arrives tonight.

Why Laplace fits this problem

Every piece of the problem is linear with constant coefficients once the film coefficients are fixed. Every input is a ramp, a step, or a sum of shifted ramps and steps. That gives the Laplace method three tools that do almost all of the work:

  1. The derivative becomes multiplication. The slab's heat equation, a PDE in depth and time, turns into an ODE in depth with \(s\) as a parameter, and that ODE has an exact solution.
  2. Delays become exponentials. A temperature kink at time \(t_k\), or the sun clearing the roofline at \(t_s\), is a factor \(e^{-t_k s}\). The second shift theorem undoes it.
  3. Superposition. Each route is its own transfer function, so each route's share of the answer can be read off separately.

1 · Room-air node

Write every temperature as a deviation \(\theta\) from 77 °F, the 06:00 state. The room, its furnishings and drywall form one node with effective capacitance \(C_a\). The balance on that node is

\[ C_a\,\dot\theta_a = A_f\,q_f(t) + K\,[\theta_o(t)-\theta_a] + Q(t),\qquad K = UA_{\text{glass}}+UA_{\text{wall}}+UA_{\text{infil}} \]

Here \(q_f\) is the heat flux up through the floor and \(Q\) collects the dogs, diffuse daylight, and the courtyard term. Because everything starts at the same temperature, the transform is just \(C_a s\,\Theta_a\). The section on initial conditions below covers a room that starts warmer.

2 · Route 1: the floor slab as an exact two-port

Inside the slab, \(\partial_t\theta=\alpha\,\partial_{zz}\theta\). Transforming in time gives \(\Theta''=(s/\alpha)\Theta\), whose solutions are \(\cosh\) and \(\sinh\) of \(\gamma z\) with \(\gamma=\sqrt{s/\alpha}\). Tracking the temperature and the flux \(\Phi=-k\Theta'\) at both faces turns the slab into a 2×2 transfer matrix. This is the thermal-quadrupole method. Each air film is a series resistance, and the layers multiply:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}\Theta_o\\ \Phi\end{bmatrix}_{\text{garage}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&\tfrac1{h_g}\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{garage film}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\gamma d & \dfrac{\sinh\gamma d}{k\gamma}\\[4pt] k\gamma\sinh\gamma d & \cosh\gamma d\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{8 in concrete}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&R_{\text{fin}}+\tfrac1{h_{in}}\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{floor finish + room film}} \begin{bmatrix}\Theta_a\\ \Phi_a\end{bmatrix} \equiv \begin{bmatrix}\mathcal A&\mathcal B\\ \mathcal C&\mathcal D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Theta_a\\ \Phi_a\end{bmatrix} \]

The top row gives the floor flux into the room directly, \(\Phi_a=(\Theta_o-\mathcal A\,\Theta_a)/\mathcal B\). Substituting it into the room balance gives two exact transfer functions:

\[ \Theta_a(s) = \underbrace{\frac{A_f/\mathcal B + K}{C_a s + A_f\mathcal A/\mathcal B + K}}_{G_o(s)}\,\Theta_o(s) \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{C_a s + A_f\mathcal A/\mathcal B + K}}_{G_Q(s)}\,Q(s) \]

As a check, when \(s\to0\) we get \(\mathcal A\to1\) and \(\mathcal B\to R_{\text{total}}\), so \(G_o(0)=1\). Given enough time, the room reaches the outdoor temperature.

3 · The inputs as shifted ramps and steps

The forecast is piecewise linear, so it is a sum of ramps that switch on at each hourly kink, where \(\Delta m_k\) is the change in slope at \(t_k\):

\[ \theta_o(t)=\sum_k \Delta m_k\,(t-t_k)\,H(t-t_k) \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Theta_o(s)=\sum_k \Delta m_k\,\frac{e^{-t_k s}}{s^2} \]

The other inputs follow the same pattern. The dogs are a step, \(Q_d/s\). Diffuse daylight ramps up from sunrise to 9:30 and then holds. The courtyard sun is a delayed step, \(q_\odot\,e^{-t_s s}/s\).

4 · Inversion with the second shift theorem

I invert only the undelayed unit responses once, for example \(r(t)=\mathcal L^{-1}\{G_o(s)/s^2\}\). Then the answer is assembled by shifting:

\[ \theta_a(t)=\sum_k \Delta m_k\, r(t-t_k)\,H(t-t_k) \;+\; Q_d\,u_Q(t) \;+\;\cdots,\qquad \mathcal L^{-1}\{e^{-as}F(s)\}=f(t-a)H(t-a) \]

\(G_o\) contains \(\cosh\sqrt{s}\), so it has infinitely many poles and is easier to invert numerically. I use Talbot's contour method from mpmath. It is accurate to many digits for functions like this, as long as no \(e^{-as}\) factor is left inside the integral. Pulling the delays out first, as above, is what makes that work.

5 · A lumped version, inverted by hand

If the slab is collapsed into a single node \(C_s\), splitting its resistance half to each face, \(G(s)\) becomes a rational function with two real poles. Every response is then a short sum of residues:

\[ \mathcal L^{-1}\!\Big\{\tfrac{G(s)}{s^2}\Big\} = G(0)\,t + G'(0) + \sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{r_i}{p_i^2}e^{p_i t},\qquad r_i = \frac{N(p_i)}{D'(p_i)} \]

The poles give the system's two time constants. τ₁ ≈ 1.6 h is the room trading heat with the slab. τ₂ ≈ 16.7 h is the whole slab-and-room system following the outdoors. The second one is why the room lags so much. The lumped model reads about 1.5 °F high by 3 pm, though. With Biot number ≈ 1.3 and Fourier number < 1 over the day, an 8-inch slab is not thermally thin, and the exact two-port matters.

6 · Route 2: sun on the courtyard slab, lateral conduction

Treat the podium slab as a fin along the horizontal coordinate \(x\). This lumps it through its thickness, which overstates how fast heat spreads sideways, so the result is an upper bound. The sunlit courtyard is \(x<0\) and the shaded interior is \(x>0\). Transforming in time gives

\[ \Theta''-m_i^2\Theta = -\frac{q_\odot\,e^{-t_s s}}{s\,kd}\ \ (x<0),\qquad m_i^2=\frac{s}{\alpha}+\frac{H_i}{kd} \]

Here \(H_i\) is the total surface conductance per unit area of slab in each region. Matching temperature and flux at \(x=0\), the flux reaching the unit's edge a distance \(L\) away is

\[ q'(s)=\frac{q_\odot\,e^{-t_s s}}{s}\;\frac{m_2\,e^{-m_2 L}}{m_1(m_1+m_2)} \;\;\xrightarrow{\ H\to0,\ L=0\ }\;\; q'(t)=q_\odot\sqrt{\alpha\,(t-t_s)/\pi} \]

The heat only penetrates a distance on the order of \(\sqrt{\alpha t}\approx\) 0.1–0.15 m into concrete over a working day. With the sun clearing the building at an assumed 11:00 and 414 W/m² absorbed, a courtyard that shares a 15 ft edge directly with your slab delivers at most about 85 W by 3 pm. If even one neighboring unit's floor sits between the courtyard and yours (L = 25 ft), the factor \(e^{-m_2 L}\approx e^{-90}\), and the courtyard contributes zero today. Since you describe the courtyard as southwest of the unit, with units to the west and south, the second case is the likely one.

7 · Route 3: walls, glass and wind

The insulated walls carry little mass, so I treat them as pure conductances. Wind enters in three places, each through a constant coefficient:

  • the exterior film \(h_o=5.7+3.8\,v_{\text{loc}}\), with \(v_{\text{loc}}=0.4\,v_{10}\) because north and east faces are leeward of a west wind;
  • the garage-side film \(h_g\), with garage air moving at 30% of the 10 m wind;
  • infiltration, \(\text{ACH}=0.20+0.05\,v_{10}\).

At the forecast 3.4 m/s this gives \(K\) = 44.6 (glass) + 17.2 (walls) + 31.1 (infiltration) = 92.9 W/K. Glass and air leakage dominate. The R-13 walls are minor.

I added one more gain the original list leaves out, labeled 3b. The unit gets no direct sun, but the north and east windows still see the sky. At about 70 W/m² of diffuse light on vertical glass and a solar heat-gain coefficient of 0.55, roughly 770 W comes through the glazing. Over the day that is the largest single gain. Closing the blinds is the main thing you control.

8 · Initial conditions

If the room starts warmer than the outdoors, the derivative transforms to \(C_a(s\Theta_a-\theta_0)\). That adds one extra term, \(C_a\theta_0\,G_Q(s)\), with no other change. A room that starts 3 °F warm loses most of that excess through the fast 1.6 h mode into the slab and ends only 0.2 °F warmer at 3 pm. A slab that is already warm from earlier hot days would matter more, because it removes the floor's sink effect. That is the biggest uncertainty here.
